1 · What is Light?
LightA form of energy that travels as a transverse electromagnetic wave. Does not need a medium — can travel through a vacuum.
Speed of light300,000,000 m/s (3 × 10⁸ m/s) in a vacuum. Slows down when it enters a denser medium (e.g. glass or water).
Ray of lightA thin beam of light shown as a straight line with an arrow showing the direction of travel.
Luminous objectsObjects that produce their own light (e.g. Sun, flame, light bulb, stars).
Non-luminous objectsObjects that are seen because they reflect light from a luminous source (e.g. Moon, book, your hand).
OpaqueDoes not transmit light — light cannot pass through. Example: wood, metal, your hand. Casts a shadow.
TransparentLight passes through clearly — you can see through it. Example: glass, clear water, perspex.
TranslucentSome light passes through but is scattered — you cannot see clearly through it. Example: frosted glass, tissue paper.
ShadowFormed when an opaque object blocks light. Same shape as the object. Umbra = full shadow; penumbra = partial shadow.
Light travels in straight lines. This is why shadows have sharp edges (with a point source of light) and why you cannot see around corners.
2 · Reflection
The Law of Reflection
The Law of Reflection
Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection
NormalA line drawn perpendicular (at 90°) to the mirror surface at the point where the ray hits. All angles are measured FROM the normal, not the surface.
Angle of incidence (i)Angle between the incoming (incident) ray and the normal.
Angle of reflection (r)Angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
Incident rayThe ray travelling towards the mirror surface.
Reflected rayThe ray bouncing back from the mirror surface.
Reflection diagram

i = angle of incidence · r = angle of reflection
i always equals r

⚠ Angles are ALWAYS measured from the normal, NOT from the mirror surface. A very common mistake is to measure from the mirror itself — this gives the wrong angle.
Types of reflection

Regular (specular) reflection

Occurs from a smooth, flat surface (e.g. plane mirror, still water, polished metal). All rays are reflected at the same angle — you see a clear image.

Diffuse reflection

Occurs from a rough, uneven surface (e.g. paper, wall, grass). Rays are reflected in many different directions. No clear image — you see a dull, matte surface. The law of reflection still applies at each point, but the surface is uneven.

Images in plane (flat) mirrors
Properties of a mirror image
  • Virtual — the image appears to be behind the mirror but no light actually travels there
  • Same size as the object
  • Same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front
  • Upright — the right way up (not inverted vertically)
  • Laterally inverted — left and right are swapped (your right hand appears as the left hand in the mirror)
Locating an image in a mirror

Object distance = Image distance from mirror

3 · Refraction
What is refraction?

Refraction is the change in direction (bending) of a light ray as it crosses the boundary between two different materials (media), caused by the change in speed of light.

Why does it happen?Light travels at different speeds in different materials. In air it travels faster than in glass or water. When it slows down at an angle, it bends towards the normal.
NormalThe same perpendicular line used in reflection — all angles measured from this line.
Angle of incidenceAngle between incoming ray and the normal (in air).
Angle of refractionAngle between refracted ray and the normal (inside the denser material).
Air → Glass (denser)Light SLOWS DOWN → bends TOWARDS the normal. Angle of refraction < angle of incidence.
Glass → Air (less dense)Light SPEEDS UP → bends AWAY from the normal. Angle of refraction > angle of incidence.
Along the normal (0°)If light hits the boundary at exactly 90° (along the normal), it passes straight through with NO bending.
Emergent rayThe ray leaving the glass block — it is parallel to the original incident ray but displaced sideways.

Refraction — real-life examples
Straw in waterThe straw appears bent/broken at the water surface. Light from the straw bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, making the straw appear to be in a different position.
Apparent depthA swimming pool appears shallower than it really is. Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it exits the water — your brain traces it back in a straight line to a point that is higher than the real bottom.
RainbowWhite light refracts as it enters and exits raindrops. Different colours refract by different amounts — they separate. Red bends least; violet bends most.
LensesConvex lenses converge (focus) parallel light rays to a point. Work by refraction at both curved surfaces. Used in glasses, cameras, microscopes.
4 · White Light & the Visible Spectrum
Dispersion of white light

White light is actually a mixture of all the colours of the visible spectrum. When white light passes through a prism (or raindrop), it is dispersed — split into its component colours — because each colour has a slightly different wavelength and therefore refracts by a slightly different amount.

Order of coloursRoy G. Biv — Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Violet
Red lightLongest wavelength (~700 nm). Lowest frequency. Refracts LEAST. At the top of the rainbow.
Violet lightShortest wavelength (~400 nm). Highest frequency. Refracts MOST. At the bottom of the rainbow.
RecombiningIf the spectrum is recombined (using a second prism inverted, or a converging lens), white light is produced again.

Colour of objects — absorption and reflection
Why objects appear coloured

Objects appear a certain colour because they reflect that colour of light and absorb all other colours. The colour we see is the colour that is reflected into our eyes.

Red objectReflects red light only. Absorbs all other colours (orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet).
White objectReflects ALL colours of light equally. Absorbs none. Appears white in white light.
Black objectAbsorbs ALL colours of light. Reflects none. Appears black.
Green object in red lightAppears BLACK — no green light to reflect; the red light is all absorbed by the green object.
Blue object in white lightReflects blue; absorbs red, orange, yellow, green, indigo, violet. Appears blue.
Red object in blue lightAppears BLACK — no red light to reflect; the blue light is absorbed.
White object in red lightAppears RED — reflects all colours it receives; the only colour present is red, so it reflects red.
FiltersA coloured filter only transmits its own colour. A red filter transmits red and absorbs all other colours.
⚠ An object in coloured light can ONLY reflect colours that are present in the light. If none of its colour is present in the light shining on it, it will appear BLACK.
Colour addition — light mixing

Primary colours of light

Red, Green and Blue (RGB). Mix all three → White light.

Red + Green= Yellow
Red + Blue= Magenta
Green + Blue= Cyan
Red + Green + Blue= White

Secondary colours of light

Yellow, Cyan and Magenta — each made by mixing two primary colours.

Adding colours of light is different from mixing paints. Mixing all paints gives dark brown/black; mixing all colours of light gives white.
TV screens and phone displays use tiny red, green and blue pixels at different brightnesses to create all the colours you see.
